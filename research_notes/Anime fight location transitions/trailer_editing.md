## 1. Which editing devices carry action across locations without it feeling disjointed?

### Takeaway
The documented tools that keep a fight continuous across spaces are: match-on-action / cut on action (the movement starts in one space and finishes in another), graphic match, a sound-led "snap" transition (the bell-and-reverse whoosh in Everything Everywhere All at Once), white-flash or impact-frame transitions (God of War 2018 hid area changes with a flash to white), and one-take camera sequences with hidden seams (God of War, John Wick: Chapter 4's top-down shot). The common rule is that the *viewer's attention and the motion vector* stay continuous even when the place changes. Sound often carries the cut as much as the picture does.

### Cited Findings
- **Match on action (cut on action)** is defined as a cut that carries an action across two shots. It is "a key element of continuity editing", and the eye follows the movement rather than noticing the cut. — [StudioBinder: What is a match on action cut](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-a-match-on-action-cut/)
- **Match on action across a location change: *The Graduate* (1967).** Benjamin "pulls himself up onto an inflatable raft in a swimming pool but when we cut, he lands somewhere else" (on Mrs Robinson in a hotel bed). This is the canonical example of a single motion started in place A and completed in place B. It is the direct model for "a swing in the courtyard lands in the rain street." — [StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-a-match-on-action-cut/)
- **Graphic match.** It is "a more conspicuous transition between two shots via pictorial elements". Examples: the bone-to-spacecraft cut in *2001: A Space Odyssey*, and the young-to-present Homer cut in The Simpsons' "Bart Star". The cut can be combined with a dissolve. — [Wikipedia: Match cut](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Match_cut); [StudioBinder: match cuts](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/match-cuts-creative-transitions-examples/)
- **Sound-led location snaps: *Everything Everywhere All at Once* (2022, editor Paul Rogers).** "We ended up using a little bell ring and reverse that would lead you and then snap you into a new universe." The team "experimented with how we hold the user's hands as we start making these transitions" so audiences wouldn't get lost. Rogers was "doing so many fight scenes and a lot of crazy multiverse transitions." He used per-shot time-remap keyframes ("as opposed to a universal change"), ramping speeds with "a push-in and the aspect ratio changing". On a laundromat fight beat: "All of a sudden you feel like you're in a Kung Fu movie and then you're out of it… A lot of that is done with sound design, but also picture editing." He also stressed planting clues early so later jumps don't feel abrupt. — [Frame.io, Art of the Cut: EEAAO (2022)](https://blog.frame.io/2022/04/13/art-of-the-cut-everything-everywhere-all-at-once/)
- **One-take camera with white-flash seams: God of War (2018, Sony Santa Monica, dir. Cory Barlog).** The game was announced as having no camera cuts. In practice the action is unbroken "except in death and the occasional flash to white when transitioning to a specific area". Barlog called the system "an absurd amount of planning and technical tricks". It was a hard sell internally, and he pitched the idea first for Tomb Raider (2013), where it was rejected. Splinter Cell: Conviction (2010) is cited as an earlier no-cut game. — [Critics at Large (2018)](https://www.criticsatlarge.ca/2018/05/prose-poem-power-sony-santa-monicas-god.html); [Game Developer: Barlog on single-shot camera](https://gamedeveloper.com/production/barlog-i-god-of-war-i-s-single-shot-camera-trick-was-a-tough-sell-for-devs); [VideoGamer: no camera cuts](https://www.videogamer.com/news/the-new-god-of-war-will-have-no-camera-cuts-whatsoever/); [GINX](https://www.ginx.tv/en/?p=37472)
- **One-take with one hidden seam: John Wick: Chapter 4 (2023), the Dragon's Breath top-down sequence.** DP Dan Laustsen said it was "basically conceived as one take, shot in a studio we built in Germany… one crane shot and one spider cam shot", with VFX help. Director Chad Stahelski: "There's only one seam in there, and that's before we light the guys on fire in the kitchen… The rest is all one take." The inspiration was the game *Hong Kong Massacre*, not Hotline Miami as fans assumed. — [SlashFilm: Stahelski on top shot](https://www.slashfilm.com/1224205/john-wick-4-director-breaks-down-that-fiery-top-shot-sequence-interview); [SlashFilm: Hong Kong Massacre inspiration](https://www.slashfilm.com/1237396/john-wick-4-director-was-inspired-by-video-game-hong-kong-massacre-for-a-key-action-scene-interview); [TheWrap: Laustsen interview](https://www.thewrap.com/john-wick-chapter-4-cinematographer-dan-laustsen-interview/)
- **Impact frames (anime).** Ufotable inserts "a particularly contrasted frame at the moment of impact". Animator Nozomu Abe uses it constantly, "creating contrast thanks to the silhouettes". The camera is also bumped in the direction of each strike, adding artificial motion blur. — [J.B. Siraudin, "Demon Slayer visual grammar"](https://jbsiraudin.github.io/blog/demon-slayer-visual-grammar)
- **Murch's priority order for any cut:** Emotion 51%, Story 23%, Rhythm 10%, Eye-trace 7%, 2D plane of screen 5%, 3D space 4%. When a cut can't satisfy all six, give up spatial continuity first and emotion last. "If the emotion is right and the story is advanced… in the right rhythm, the audience will tend to be unaware of (or unconcerned about) editorial choices." — [UT Austin wiki: Murch's Rule of Six](https://cloud.wikis.utexas.edu/wiki/spaces/rtf318/pages/80022510/Murch+s+Rule+of+Six); [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/11/6-rules-good-cutting-according-oscar-winning-editor-walter-murch); [Artlist: eye trace and rule of six](https://artlist.io/blog/eye-trace-and-rule-of-six-editing/)

### Inferences
- Murch's ranking is the theoretical licence for this trailer: 3D spatial continuity is the *least* important criterion. Teleporting Haru and Mitsuki from courtyard to street is acceptable if emotion, rhythm and eye trace carry across the cut. The current floaty leaps are an attempt to preserve 3D continuity, the lowest priority, at the cost of rhythm, a higher one.
- **Device-to-transition mapping for the four locations** (my synthesis, not documented):
  1. *Courtyard → rain street:* match on action, the Graduate model. Haru's downward diagonal slash starts in sunlight and the blade completes the arc in rain at night. Keep the blade's screen position and direction identical across the cut (eye trace) and let the sword-clash sound carry over the cut (an audio pre-lap, EEAAO-style).
  2. *Rain street → burning rooftops:* smash cut on impact plus an impact frame. A clash produces a 1–2 frame high-contrast or white frame, and the next shot is already on the rooftops mid-exchange. The God of War precedent uses a white flash to hide an area change.
  3. *Rooftops → infinite castle:* graphic match or "knock-through". Mitsuki is driven through a burning shoji or roof panel. The frame fills with fire or debris, which acts as a natural wipe, and the camera emerges inside the castle with the fighters falling past floors.
  4. A *cross-cut exchange* at the climax: alternate 4–8-frame shots of the same duel in all four locations, cut on each beat of the music. This "one fight, many places" montage reads as one fight because the choreography continues shot to shot.
- **Whip pan as a transition:** a fast pan that blurs into a matching blur in the new location. It is widely used (for example by Edgar Wright and in many anime OPs), but I found no citable source this session. See Gaps.

### Gaps
- No source found this session specifically on whip-pan transitions, split screens in game trailers, or a named game trailer that cross-cuts one fight across locations. Candidates to verify by viewing: Dragon Ball Sparking! Zero trailers, the Naruto Storm 4 "Naruto vs Sasuke" trailers, and Demon Slayer anime OPs.
- The full Paul Rogers interviews (Editor's Cut ep. 078, Art of the Cut podcast ep. 157) likely discuss match cuts in the "fanny pack fight". Not verified.

- **Location establishing in anime-game trailers (recollection):** the CyberConnect2 trailers (Naruto Storm 4, Hinokami Chronicles) usually introduce an arena *with the fight already in frame*. - **Ghost of Tsushima / Sekiro (recollection):** Ghost's trailers lean on long held wides with wind, grass, falling leaves and a silhouette standoff. They contrast stillness with sudden fast cuts on the draw. Sekiro trailers use rooftop and grapple traversal between vantage points, so the movement through space is itself the transition. Both are useful models for the "breath" and for silhouette framing.
- **Wide-to-close ratio:** no figure is documented. As a working assumption for a 65 s anime-style trailer, about 30–40% wides (environment-reading shots), 40–50% mediums and two-shots, and 15–25% inserts and close-ups (eyes, blade edge, feet, sparks) is my estimate, to be validated by measuring 3–4 reference trailers.

## 3. Shot vocabulary for 3D fights that shows off the environment

### Takeaway
The documented vocabulary is: ufotable-style 3D camera moves (tracking, orbiting, moving *with* the environment) around center-framed action; the top-down "god's-eye" one-take (John Wick 4, borrowed from Hong Kong Massacre) that turns the architecture into a readable map; silhouette-driven impact frames; and a camera shake animated at a higher rate than the characters. Center-framing keeps fast cuts readable while the background does the location-selling.

### Cited Findings
- **Top-down / overhead tracking one-take:** John Wick 4's Dragon's Breath sequence was shot overhead on crane and spidercam across a built set of rooms. The camera glides over walls so the building's floor plan *is* the composition. It came from the top-down game Hong Kong Massacre. — [SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/1237396/john-wick-4-director-was-inspired-by-video-game-hong-kong-massacre-for-a-key-action-scene-interview); [TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/john-wick-chapter-4-cinematographer-dan-laustsen-interview/)
- **Center-compressed framing for fast action**, so the eye doesn't travel between cuts (ufotable, following Mad Max: Fury Road). — [Siraudin](https://jbsiraudin.github.io/blog/demon-slayer-visual-grammar)
- **Directional camera bump on impact**, plus camera shake at 24 fps over 12 fps animation. — [Siraudin](https://jbsiraudin.github.io/blog/demon-slayer-visual-grammar)
- **Silhouette contrast in impact frames** (Nozomu Abe). — [Siraudin](https://jbsiraudin.github.io/blog/demon-slayer-visual-grammar)
- **The 3D environment as an "arena" that the camera explores** is the key to Demon Slayer's action cinematography, according to Wrong Every Time / Crunchyroll. The Infinity Castle background scale (about 2 km² of painted 3D) was built specifically to give camera freedom. — [Wrong Every Time](https://wrongeverytime.com/2019/05/06/why-it-works-the-digital-arenas-of-demon-slayers-battles); [Popverse/Terao](https://www.thepopverse.com/movies-demon-slayer-kimetsu-no-yaiba-yuichi-terao-interview-making-the-infinity-castle-feel-infinite)

### Inferences (shot list per location: my synthesis)
- **Temple courtyard (day):** low angle with sky and the pagoda roofline behind crossed blades. A foreground-occlusion dolly past stone lanterns or a torii pillar with the fight behind (parallax sells depth and place in under 1 s). A top-down shot onto raked gravel, where the fighters' footwork carves the pattern, is a strong graphic image that doubles as a "map".
- **Rainy Taisho street (night):** a puddle-reflection shot, inverted, of the clash with gas lamps. Framing through a shop window or noren curtain. A long lateral tracking shot alongside both fighters running and exchanging blows down the street, with lamps and signage whipping past in the foreground. Rain stopping in slow motion for the breath.
- **Burning rooftops:** silhouettes against fire, with a backlit two-shot on the ridge line. A drone-like pull-back from a blade lock to reveal the whole burning district. A low tracking shot along the ridge tiles as they run.
- **Infinite castle:** a top-down or bottom-up vertigo shot down a shaft of stacked rooms. A rotating camera as gravity flips. Fighters falling *with* the camera, a static relative framing while the world scrolls (consistent with "the camera moves with the platforms"). Framing through receding sliding doors.
- **POV:** one or two 6–10 frame POV shots, such as the blade coming at the lens, can act as the hinge cut into a new location (the next shot is the reverse, in the new place).

### Gaps
- No documented source found on puddle-reflection, window-framing or POV conventions in game trailers specifically. These are standard cinematography, but no citation was collected this session.

## 4. Pacing and rhythm: music-cut relationship, cut rate, where to place the breath, building to a climax

### Takeaway
Feature-film data puts action at about 4 s average shot length, but trailers run faster. No reliable trailer-specific figure was found. Derek Lieu, a professional game trailer editor, argues that more cuts is not better: cut to the beat by feel (sometimes two frames off the exact beat looks more in sync), use roughly 4-frame audio crossfades between game clips, and let shots breathe. Ufotable's prepare-strike-relax rhythm and its use of slow motion as a "breathing moment" give a micro-structure for each exchange.

### Cited Findings
- **Average shot length (feature films):** action ≈ 4.0 s, adventure 5.1 s, sci-fi 6.2 s, horror ≈ 15.7 s. Action films have the most shots (average 1,913). — [Stephen Follows: how many shots in the average movie](https://stephenfollows.com/many-shots-average-movie/)
- **Trailer length:** the average across about 6,926 trailers from 1930–2025 is 122.8 s. This measures length, not cut rate. — [Stephen Follows](https://stephenfollows.com/p/have-movie-trailers-always-been-this-long)
- Claims of "2.1 cuts per second" in trailers come from an unverifiable secondary source, so treat them as unreliable. — [Dyerbolical](https://dyerbolical.com/why-movie-trailers-are-getting-shorter-and-faster-the-real-reasons-behind-the-shift/) (flagged unreliable). Vashi Visuals cites a typical action ASL of about 2 s with no stated method. — [Vashi Visuals](https://vashivisuals.com/the-fastest_cut/)
- **Derek Lieu (game trailer editor):** "MORE editing does not mean BETTER editing". Cut at a pace that suits the game and the feeling you want, and longer takes can feel more authentic than many short clips. — [Derek Lieu blog: "More trailer editing is not better trailer editing" (2023)](https://derek-lieu-yrd2.squarespace.com/blog/2023/4/23/more-trailer-editing-is-not-better-trailer-editing)
- **Derek Lieu on music sync:** he taps to the beat while playing back and stops where a cut should go. "Trust your feeling of where the cuts and sync points should land." "Sometimes two frames off looks more in sync than what's in sync." He uses about 4-frame audio transitions between consecutive game clips that carry their own audio, and orders trailer SFX (whooshes, hits, booms) on their own track. — [Derek Lieu: "Professional video editing tidbits" (2024)](https://derek-lieu-yrd2.squarespace.com/blog/2024/5/5/professional-video-editing-tidbits)
- **Strike rhythm:** prepare (long), strike (short), relax (long). Slow motion is used as a breathing moment in hectic scenes. — [Siraudin](https://jbsiraudin.github.io/blog/demon-slayer-visual-grammar)
- **Tonal compression:** "Within a thirty second span we're trying to make you cry and then laugh and then be scared" (Rogers on EEAAO). Speed ramps are keyframed per shot. — [Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2022/04/13/art-of-the-cut-everything-everywhere-all-at-once/)
- **Rhythm ranks third in Murch's priorities**, after emotion and story. — [UT Austin wiki](https://cloud.wikis.utexas.edu/wiki/spaces/rtf318/pages/80022510/Murch+s+Rule+of+Six)

### Inferences
- **A proposed 65 s intensity map** (my synthesis, to make "transitions in fighting intensity" structural):
  - 0–14 s, *Courtyard, day (sparring, measured):* longer shots of 1.5–3 s, more wides, clean exchanges, prepare-strike-relax readable. Ends on a match-on-action slash into rain.
  - 14–30 s, *Rain street, night (escalating):* about 1–1.5 s shots, tracking runs, reflections. Mid-point **breath** around 24–27 s: rain hangs in slow motion, a held two-shot or silhouette, near-silence or a music drop. A clash then smash-cuts to fire.
  - 30–46 s, *Burning rooftops (heated):* 0.5–1 s shots, silhouettes, a pull-back reveal. Ends on a knock-through into the castle.
  - 46–60 s, *Infinite castle (climax):* fastest cutting, 6–12-frame shots, with a 3–4 s **cross-cut montage** flashing back through all four locations on consecutive beats. Final clash, then a white impact frame.
  - 60–65 s: title card or held final image.
- Cut on downbeats for location changes and on off-beats or accents for strikes inside a location. Per Lieu, nudge cuts by feel, up to about 2 frames early.
- The breath should sit just *before* the steepest escalation (the end of act 2), not at the very end. A second micro-breath of 0.5–1 s of freeze or silence right before the final hit is a common trailer device (recollection, unverified).

## 5. Edit-level answers to "how do we show all the locations without characters flying?"

### Takeaway
Change location *on the cut, not in the shot*. Start a motion in place A and finish it in place B (match on action), let an impact or flash hide the jump (God of War white flash, ufotable impact frames), use sound to "snap" the viewer into the new space (EEAAO bell-and-reverse), and enter each new location mid-exchange. Inside a location, move the fight along surfaces (running, sliding, wall-running, rooftop sprints, falling) rather than through the air.

### Cited Findings
- A motion that starts in one place and lands in another across a cut is a documented, accepted convention (The Graduate's raft-to-bed cut). — [StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-a-match-on-action-cut/)
- Flash-to-white used specifically to cover area transitions in an otherwise continuous-camera action game (God of War 2018). — [Critics at Large](https://www.criticsatlarge.ca/2018/05/prose-poem-power-sony-santa-monicas-god.html)
- Sound pre-lap and snap transitions guide viewers through location jumps; the EEAAO team tested how to "hold the user's hands". — [Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2022/04/13/art-of-the-cut-everything-everywhere-all-at-once/)
- A continuous camera can travel through multiple rooms of a fight by tracking over architecture (John Wick 4 top-down) instead of following characters through the air. Seams are hidden at natural occlusion or flare moments ("before we light the guys on fire"). — [SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/1224205/john-wick-4-director-breaks-down-that-fiery-top-shot-sequence-interview)
- Spatial (3D) continuity is the lowest-priority criterion for a cut. — [Murch via UT Austin wiki](https://cloud.wikis.utexas.edu/wiki/spaces/rtf318/pages/80022510/Murch+s+Rule+of+Six)

### Inferences (concrete fixes for the Haru/Mitsuki trailer)
- **Delete every on-screen two-person leap.** Replace it with one of the following:
  1. *Cut on action:* frame the takeoff (crouch, push off, 6–8 frames), cut, then frame the landing or strike in the new spot. The airborne part happens off-screen.
  2. *Fight already under way:* the first shot in each new location is mid-clash (blade lock, parry spark), never an arrival.
  3. *Ground travel:* a running exchange down the rain street (lateral track), sliding on wet stone, sprinting the rooftop ridge, running down a castle corridor that tilts into a wall run.
  4. *Knock-through:* a kick or clash sends Mitsuki through a shoji, gate or roof panel. Debris or fire fills the frame (a natural wipe), and on the other side is a new space. This works inside a location too: courtyard gate → temple hall.
  5. *Falling, not flying:* in the infinite castle, falling is diegetic. Lock the camera to the fighters while rooms scroll past (moving with the environment).
  6. *Impact-frame jump:* a high-contrast silhouette or white frame on a clash, 1–3 frames, then the new location.
- **Use one consistent screen direction per transition.** For example, Haru always attacks left-to-right, so eye trace survives every location change (Murch's eye trace and 2D plane criteria).
- **Carry audio across the cuts:** the blade ring and rain hiss lead into each new location by 4–8 frames, using J-cuts.
- Floaty leaps also look wrong because they hold the viewer on the weakest animation. Cutting *into* the leap's apex or landing keeps only the strongest frames, consistent with ufotable's "strike is one frame, brutal pose change".

### Gaps
- No interview found in which a game trailer editor explicitly discusses hiding traversal between arenas. The fixes above are synthesis from film and anime technique plus the God of War precedent.
